Why trades go wrong

You are not only paying for the card. You are paying for the risk.

Most platforms bundle condition disputes, payment handling, fraud gaps, trust scaffolding, and shipping into one fee. This protocol separates those costs so the trade can be priced around the actual card, seller, route, and proof in front of you.

The real question is not "is this platform safe?" It is: who holds the money, what was claimed before the trade closed, and what happens if the card is not what the seller said?

Fees eat the spread

You pay a blended platform rate even when your trade needs only a small amount of hand-holding.

Trust is trapped

Seller reputation often lives inside one marketplace. Proofs, bonds, receipts, and prior clean closes should travel with the seller.

Evidence is scattered

Photos, DMs, tracking, insurance, and condition claims should not be reconstructed after something goes wrong.

Disputes are expensive

If the record is clean before the card ships, the ending is clearer: receipt, claim, refund, or arbitration.

How it works for a buyer

You set the rules. The agent runs the search.

Tell the agent the card, condition floor, price ceiling, route cost you will absorb, and when to come back. It works inside those rules and interrupts only when there is a real decision to make.

Describe the hunt

Name the exact card, or describe the era, artist, language, condition, and taste radius you are willing to consider.

Set risk rules

Decide what condition evidence you need, which seller signals count, when a bond is required, and how much fee you will bear.

Hold funds

The agent can bring serious seller responses because the buyer's money is committed, but not released until the gates are met.

Review the card

Every candidate comes back in plain English: what was found, what was claimed, what is missing, and what still needs judgment.

You decide

Ask for evidence, approve shipping, accept the risk, negotiate, meet locally, or pass. The agent does not blur judgment into protocol proof.

Use-case spectrum

From casual trust to museum-grade proof.

The protocol should not force every object through the same ritual. Sometimes the right experience is simple: you get the item roughly how you expected. Sometimes the right experience is a sealed evidence trail with verifier, insurance, bond, and careful escrow release.

Casual item

Low-value object, trusted seller, rough condition agreement, simple photos, tracked or local handoff, light receipt.

Condition matters

Mid-value collectible, visible flaws called out, focused closeups, escrow, inspection window, insured route if shipped.

New seller

Same item, lower trust. Add seller bond, delayed payout, stronger evidence, route promise, and explicit human approval.

High-end

Expensive condition-sensitive item. Add video, verifier option, insured signature route, packaging evidence, and claim packet.

Extreme proof

Grail or disputed-risk object. Verifier-forwarding, serial evidence, full route stream, strong bond, arbitration terms, final receipt.

The same rails, tuned for Pokemon cards that are hard to find and easy to misjudge.

Pokemon Alpha is the first sharp aperture: funded wantlists meet seller inventory, agents negotiate condition evidence and route risk, and escrow releases only when the card arrives the way the buyer agreed it should.

Card shows still matter. They are a route and discovery surface, not the whole protocol.

Buyer pain

The buyer wants a specific Pokemon card, maybe with taste attached: Japanese vintage, clean holo, no crease, a certain artist, a budget ceiling, and a willingness to move fast when the right binder finally opens.

  • Search is scattered across shows, shops, listings, and DMs.

  • Fees and shipping can turn a fair card into a bad buy.
  • New sellers can have the card but not yet have the trust.

The seller may have real inventory, but listing everything with full scans is labor. A seller agent can expose enough Pokemon inventory to funded buyers while protecting attention, margin, and floor.

  • Only funded intents deserve scarce photo and haggle time.
  • Hidden binder stock can surface without becoming public inventory.
  • Bonds and delayed payout help new sellers reach serious buyers.
  • Lower-fee direct rails let both sides share the saved spread.

Trust sources

Trust is measured authority, weighted from the buyer's aperture.

The seller can offer any proof they want. Signed proofs are stronger, but even loose evidence can be useful if an agent knows what it proves, who issued it, how fresh it is, and whether it matters for this exact buyer, object, route, and price.

A trust source does not say "trust this seller." It says "this evidence has authority over this narrow claim."

1. Seller offers proof

Any evidence chain is allowed: marketplace history, shop identity, verifier receipts, photos, references, route records, or economic bond.

2. Agent probes it

The buyer agent asks who signed it, what it proves, when it was observed, whether it can be challenged, and what risk remains.

3. Buyer weights it

Each buyer aperture decides relevance. TCGplayer history may matter deeply for Pokemon cards while other categories stay outside alpha.

4. Outcomes harden it

Clean closes, claims, disputes, bond releases, and failed promises recalibrate which proofs deserve weight next time.

The hardening loop

The protocol gets stronger when evidence meets outcome. A proof starts as borrowed authority, then earns or loses weight when the seller's actions match the claim.

  • Proof offered: outside reputation, shop identity, bond, route promise.
  • Action taken: shipped, met locally, accepted verifier, answered probes.
  • Outcome recorded: clean close, claim, dispute, refund, bond release, bond hit.
  • Authority recalibrated: agents learn which proofs predict which outcomes.

Both sides

Buyers bring funded intent. Sellers bring reachable inventory.

The protocol starts before a listing exists. A buyer can expose a funded want, and a seller can expose inventory under rules that protect attention, margin, and trust.

Buyer perspective

The buyer does not need to babysit search tabs. They tell an agent what they want, what risks they will tolerate, how much fee and route cost they will bear, and when to come back for judgment.

  • Exact wants: buy this object in this condition or better under this all-in price.
  • Taste-cloud wants: adjacent items that match style, condition, era, use, or story.
  • Attention rules: ask for preference drift, price stretch, condition risk, or escrow approval.
  • Trust rules: allow new sellers only with bond, escrow, inspection, or verifier.
  • Discovery rules: show adjacent inventory only when it is worth the attention cost.

Seller perspective

The seller does not need to list every item everywhere. They publish how agents can access inventory, evidence, holds, haggling, and trust substitutes without wasting human time.

  • Listed inventory can match directly against funded buyer intents.
  • Hidden inventory can surface when a taste or fit envelope is close.
  • Evidence tiers prevent low-value proof requests from eating the day.
  • Reserve policy protects inventory from being held by unfunded buyers.
  • Bond and delayed payout let new sellers reach buyers before reputation matures.

Negotiation

The deal is the whole route, not the sticker price.

A good trade might be lower-fee, better protected, faster, more reversible, less annoying, or more valuable to both sides. Agents should be able to negotiate across all of those dimensions.

When trust is weak

A new seller can still reach the buyer by posting a bond, accepting delayed payout, sending useful closeups, routing through a verifier, or pricing below trusted comps.

When attention is expensive

The buyer agent bundles questions, skips low-value evidence rituals, and saves seller labor for uncertainty that actually changes the decision.

Bonded sellers

New sellers can buy their first lane of trust.

Bonded sellers are the bootstrap unlock. A seller with no protocol history can still reach serious buyers by collateralizing boring, specific promises: ship the item, describe it materially correctly, disclose damage, and accept a defined inspection path.

Unbonded new seller

Low visibility, small transactions first, limited agent access.

Transaction-bonded seller

Posts collateral behind one item and one evidence bundle.

Standing-bond seller

Maintains rolling collateral for broader buyer-agent access.

Trusted seller

Clean history reduces bond requirements and evidence friction.

Verifier-backed seller

High-value condition-sensitive goods can move with third-party confidence.

Route evidence

Every handoff leaves a claim-ready trail.

Tracking is one evidence type, not the whole design. The protocol should accept carrier scans, receipt photos, table handoff memos, shop intake notes, verifier attestations, and buyer confirmations as route evidence attached to the trade.

Standard shipping

For USPS, FedEx, UPS, or another carrier, the route stream can record the promise, label, insurance amount, declared value, acceptance scan, tracking events, delivery scan, signature, and inspection window.

  • Label and tracking URL are cheap evidence.
  • Insurance and signature are paid route protections.
  • Packaging attestation belongs to the insured route, even when item evidence is light.

Non-standard handoff

An event pickup, local meetup, or shop handoff can be just as protocol-legible. The evidence may be a pickup memo, shop receipt, verifier intake, signed note, or simple buyer confirmation.

  • Meet in person when locations match and the buyer wants that.
  • Use a shop or verifier when both sides want a neutral handoff.
  • Keep escrow locked until receipt and condition are confirmed.

Happy closure

When the buyer is happy, the trade becomes a receipt.

The final receipt is the happy-path mirror of a claim packet. It proves what was bought, what route was used, what condition was accepted, what escrow released, and which promises become reputation.

Buyer acceptance

The buyer or their agent confirms receipt and condition after the inspection window. That acceptance should be explicit enough to release escrow but not so public that it leaks collection habits by default.

  • Item received and matched the accepted condition band.
  • Escrow release approved after route and condition checks.
  • Receipt saved privately unless the buyer chooses to share it.

Seller closure

The seller gets paid, the bond is released or aged down, and the completed promises become future access. A clean close should make the next deal easier for everyone.

  • Payout released according to escrow terms.
  • Bond released when no dispute opens inside the window.
  • Reputation events credit accurate condition, route follow-through, and clean communication.

Resolution engine

Resolve or claim is where the protocol proves itself.

Every ending asks the same three questions: what was promised, what happened, and who knowingly accepted which route risk. The answer determines whether the agent releases escrow, emits a receipt, opens a carrier claim, holds a bond, or starts arbitration.

1. Promised

Route, insurance, signature, condition band, evidence tier, inspection window, and bond terms.

2. Happened

Carrier events, handoff notes, buyer confirmation, received condition, verifier notes, or exceptions.

3. Accepted

Which human or agent approved each exception: uninsured, underinsured, local handoff, delay, or waiver.

Why this matters

Without typed route promises, every dispute becomes vibes. With them, the agent can say exactly what kind of ending this is: clean receipt, insured carrier claim, route-risk dispute, condition dispute, bond exposure, or verifier-backed close.

  • Insurance is clean when value, route, and evidence are already attached.
  • Uninsured exceptions are only fair when the accepting party is explicit.
  • Local handoff is not informal; it is a different evidence route.

Protocol objects

Alpha API

Start as a Pokemon agent skill. Prove one trade shape first.

The first product does not need to be a giant marketplace. It needs to let agents post funded Pokemon wants, talk to seller agents, reserve one-card inventory, and make trust legible enough to transact.

1. Post intent

Buyer agent publishes funded Pokemon-card intent with card, condition floor, taste, budget, trust, route, and attention gates.

2. Open inventory

Seller agent exposes listed and hidden Pokemon card claims with condition, price, evidence tier, and access policy.

3. Compare maps

The matcher checks price, taste, trust, route, evidence, time, and reversibility without flattening the trade.

4. Haggle the route

Agents trade bond, closeups, reserve duration, inspection, local handoff, shipping, price, and attention boundaries.

5. Lock escrow

Funds, seller bond, item evidence, route evidence, and release conditions become typed commitments.

6. Resolve or claim

Happy acceptance produces a final receipt; shipping damage or loss produces an agent-ready claim packet.

Design rule: a transaction should never say "trust score 0.71, proceed." It should say "this is worth chasing, the seller is new, and bond plus escrow plus the right evidence make it accountable enough to ask you."
